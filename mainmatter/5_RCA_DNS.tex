\chapter{Geographic Regionalization of Anycast Services: Region-Constrained Anycast over DNS (RCA-DNS)}
\label{ch:rcadns}

As demonstrated throughout the previous chapters, anycast infrastructures have become a fundamental component of modern Internet services. Content Delivery Networks (CDNs), cloud providers, Domain Name System (DNS) operators, and numerous large-scale online platforms rely on anycast deployments to improve latency, increase resilience, and distribute traffic among geographically distributed service replicas. These properties have contributed significantly to the widespread adoption of anycast as a core mechanism for service delivery on the Internet.

However, the same characteristics that make anycast attractive from a performance and availability perspective also introduce important challenges regarding geographic transparency and control. Since routing decisions are dynamically determined by the inter-domain routing system, the physical destination of a communication cannot be directly inferred from the destination IP address alone. Furthermore, the geographic location reached by a communication may vary depending on the origin network, routing policies, peering agreements, traffic engineering decisions, and transient network conditions.

The methodology presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:hunter} addressed this problem by enabling the inference of the geographic destinations associated with anycast communications. Using active network measurements, traceroute analysis, and latency-based multilateration, Hunter demonstrated that it is possible to estimate the countries where anycast replicas are deployed with high precision. Building upon this capability, Chapter~\ref{ch:measurements} revealed that anycast infrastructures are extensively used by Android applications and Third-Party Libraries (TPLs) when processing personal data. More importantly, the experiments showed that communications frequently cross jurisdictional boundaries and may reach countries that are not explicitly disclosed in the corresponding privacy policies.

These observations highlight a fundamental limitation of current anycast deployments: while they provide efficient mechanisms for selecting nearby replicas, they offer little control over the geographic jurisdictions reached by user communications. Consequently, organisations may face difficulties enforcing data residency requirements, complying with regulatory obligations, or guaranteeing that communications remain confined within specific geographic regions. Similar challenges arise in contexts involving data sovereignty, national regulations, contractual restrictions, or sector-specific compliance requirements.

Existing approaches to geographic traffic control typically rely on application-level filtering, DNS geolocation mechanisms, or independent regional service deployments. Although these techniques may partially mitigate the problem, they often introduce operational complexity, reduce the benefits traditionally associated with anycast, or require significant modifications to existing infrastructures. As a result, achieving both geographic control and the operational advantages of anycast remains an open challenge.

To address this problem, this chapter presents \textit{Region-Constrained Anycast over DNS (RCA-DNS)}, a mechanism designed to introduce geographic constraints into anycast service delivery while preserving the scalability, resilience, and performance characteristics that motivate the use of anycast in the first place. The key idea behind RCA-DNS is to combine DNS-based request steering with region-specific anycast deployments, allowing service providers to restrict communications to predefined geographic regions without requiring modifications to client devices or to the global routing infrastructure.

The remainder of this chapter is organised as follows. First, the motivation behind RCA-DNS and the design requirements guiding its development are presented. Next, the architecture and operation of the proposed regionalised anycast system are described in detail. Finally, an experimental evaluation is conducted to assess the effectiveness of the proposal in confining communications within predefined geographic regions while analysing its impact on service performance and operational deployment.

\section{Motivation and Design Requirements}
\label{sc:rcadns_motivation}

The results presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:anycast_experiments} demonstrate that anycast infrastructures introduce a significant loss of geographic transparency and control over Internet communications. The experiments revealed that communications involving personal data may reach different destination countries depending on the origin network, routing conditions, and the operational state of the anycast infrastructure. Furthermore, many of these destination countries are not explicitly disclosed in the corresponding privacy policies, making it difficult for users and organisations to determine where their data are actually processed.

This lack of geographic predictability creates important challenges from both technical and regulatory perspectives. From a technical standpoint, service operators have limited control over the jurisdictions reached by user traffic once routing decisions are delegated to the global inter-domain routing system. From a regulatory perspective, organisations may be unable to guarantee that communications remain within specific geographic regions, potentially leading to unintentional cross-border transfers of personal data.

The problem is particularly relevant in scenarios where geographic restrictions are required. Examples include compliance with data protection regulations, national data sovereignty requirements, contractual obligations regarding data residency, or the deployment of services intended to remain confined within specific political or administrative regions. In these situations, the traditional anycast model provides high performance and resilience but offers little control over the jurisdictions where communications are ultimately delivered.

The observations obtained throughout this thesis suggest that the challenge is not the use of anycast itself, but rather the absence of mechanisms allowing operators to constrain the geographic scope of anycast routing decisions. Existing anycast deployments are designed to optimise performance, availability, and operational efficiency, while geographic control is typically left as a secondary concern. Consequently, users located within the same region may still be routed towards replicas deployed in different countries whenever the routing system considers those paths preferable.

To address these limitations, this chapter presents \textit{Region-Constrained Anycast over DNS (RCA-DNS)}, a mechanism designed to preserve the performance benefits of anycast while introducing explicit geographic constraints on replica selection. Instead of allowing the routing system to select among all available replicas worldwide, RCA-DNS restricts the set of reachable anycast instances according to predefined geographic regions.

The design of RCA-DNS is guided by the following requirements:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compatibility with existing infrastructures.} The solution should operate using standard Internet protocols and require minimal modifications to client devices and applications.

    \item \textbf{Incremental deployability.} Service providers should be able to adopt the mechanism without requiring changes to the global routing infrastructure or coordination with third-party network operators.

    \item \textbf{Preservation of anycast benefits.} The solution should maintain the scalability, resilience, load distribution, and low-latency properties traditionally associated with anycast deployments.

    \item \textbf{Operational simplicity.} The mechanism should be manageable using existing operational practices and should not introduce excessive configuration complexity.

    \item \textbf{Support for regulatory compliance.} The resulting architecture should facilitate the implementation of geographic policies aimed at satisfying data residency, sovereignty, and privacy requirements.
\end{itemize}

Based on these requirements, RCA-DNS combines DNS-based request steering with region-specific anycast deployments, enabling service operators to constrain communications to predefined geographic areas while retaining the operational advantages of anycast infrastructures. The architecture and operation of the proposed mechanism are described in the following section.

\section{RCA-DNS Architecture}
\label{sc:rcadns_architecture}

\subsection{Architecture Overview}
\label{sc:rcadns_overview}

Region-Constrained Anycast over DNS (RCA-DNS) is designed to preserve the scalability, resilience, and performance advantages of anycast while introducing explicit geographic constraints on where communications may be processed. The central idea behind RCA-DNS is to separate two functions that are traditionally coupled in operational anycast deployments: determining the geographic region associated with a communication and selecting the infrastructure that will process the traffic. In conventional anycast systems, both decisions are implicitly delegated to Internet routing, which may direct communications towards infrastructure deployed in jurisdictions that are unrelated to the user's location or the intended regulatory domain.

RCA-DNS introduces an additional control layer based on DNS resolution. Instead of exposing a single anycast address for a service, multiple region-specific DNS records are defined, each associated with a different anycast address and a corresponding geographic region. Traffic is therefore directed towards a regional anycast deployment whose infrastructure is intentionally restricted to a predefined jurisdictional scope.

This design allows service providers to define explicit geographic boundaries while preserving the operational characteristics that make anycast attractive for large-scale distributed services. Importantly, RCA-DNS does not require modifications to Internet routing protocols, end-user devices, or application-layer communication protocols, making the approach compatible with existing Internet infrastructure.

\subsection{Regionalized Resolution and Traffic Steering}
\label{sc:rcadns_resolution}

Under RCA-DNS, each geographic region is associated with a dedicated DNS record and a dedicated anycast address. The service provider announces each anycast address exclusively from infrastructure physically deployed within the corresponding region. For example, a service may expose records such as \textit{eea.example.com}, \textit{usa.example.com}, or \textit{apac.example.com}. Although all records provide access to the same logical service, each one resolves to a different anycast address, whose announcements are geographically constrained to the corresponding region. Furthermore, a worldwide DNS record, \textit{global.example.com}, could also be used when the communications do not need to be constrained to a specific region or jurisdiction.

The moment the service initialises a communication, the service first determines the region that should be used according to the provider's policy. This decision is conceptually independent from routing and may rely on several mechanisms, including user-provided information, coarse geographic location, contractual requirements, or regulatory considerations. Once the region has been selected, the corresponding DNS record is used for subsequent communications or varies dynamically if the conditions change over time.

After DNS resolution, traffic delivery relies entirely on standard anycast routing. Since the selected anycast address is only announced from infrastructure deployed within the designated region, routing decisions remain confined to that geographic area, even though normal BGP-based anycast mechanisms continue to operate.

This separation between region determination and routing control constitutes one of the key properties of RCA-DNS. Geographic constraints are enforced at the DNS layer, while traffic engineering, load balancing, resilience, and fail-over continue to be handled through conventional anycast routing mechanisms.

From a deployment perspective, RCA-DNS requires one anycast address per region and corresponding regional DNS records. Such requirements are compatible with current Internet operational practices and can be implemented without modifications to existing DNS or routing infrastructure. Consequently, RCA-DNS offers a practical mechanism for introducing geographic control into anycast deployments while preserving compatibility with current operational environments.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sc:rcadns_evaluation}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sc:rcadns_setup}

The evaluation of RCA-DNS focuses on assessing the practical feasibility of enforcing geographic constraints in anycast-based services. In particular, the objective is to determine whether restricting traffic to servers located within a predefined geographic region can effectively reduce jurisdictional exposure while maintaining acceptable network performance.

To this end, an experimental deployment of RCA-DNS was implemented on Google Cloud. Five identical service replicas were deployed in five countries belonging to the EEA \hugo{Include the names of the countries}. Each replica hosted the same HTTP service and collectively emulated a region-constrained anycast deployment in which traffic was intentionally restricted to infrastructure located within the EEA.

Latency measurements were performed using RIPE Atlas probes distributed across the five RIPE Atlas macro-regions: Europe, America, Asia, Africa, and Oceania. Measurements were executed periodically over multiple days \hugo{Include the exact number of days}, and the observed RTT was used as the primary performance metric. RTT was selected because it captures both network propagation delay and service responsiveness; therefore, it provides an appropriate indicator of the practical cost introduced by geographic confinement.

The results presented in the following subsection evaluate the trade-off introduced by RCA-DNS between geographic control and communication performance, providing an empirical assessment of its viability as a mechanism for reducing unintended cross-border routing in anycast systems.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sc:rcadns_results}

The experimental results indicate that RCA-DNS can enforce region-aware service selection while maintaining latency levels compatible with practical Internet services. The deployed prototype successfully resolved client requests towards service replicas located within the predefined geographic region. In the evaluated scenario, all available replicas were deployed within the EEA, allowing for the assessment of the performance impact associated with restricting service selection to a bounded geographic area.

The latency measurements obtained through RIPE Atlas show that the introduction of geographic constraints does not impose prohibitive performance penalties. For measurements originating within Europe, the median RTT was 57.20~ms and decreased to 51.80~ms when only EEA countries were considered.

As expected, the lowest latency values were observed in countries hosting service replicas, where the median RTT reached 32.29~ms. Measurements originating outside Europe exhibited higher latency values due to the additional propagation delay associated with intercontinental communication paths. Nevertheless, the observed increase remained moderate and followed the expected relationship between geographic distance and network latency.

The cumulative RTT distribution further illustrates the practical viability of the approach. Within the EEA, 88.13\% of the measurements remained below 100~ms, while 94.92\% remained below 150~ms. These latency values are generally considered acceptable for interactive web services and are comparable to those observed in many commercial cloud deployments. \hugo{Include the references used in the CACM paper.}

Overall, the results suggest that introducing geographic constraints through RCA-DNS does not necessarily entail substantial degradation in user-perceived performance. Although restricting replica selection reduces the routing flexibility available to unconstrained deployments, the observed latency values remain compatible with the requirements of many practical Internet services. The evaluation, therefore, provides evidence that geographic regionalisation can be achieved with a relatively limited performance cost, supporting the feasibility of RCA-DNS as a mechanism for introducing geographic control into anycast-based service architectures.

\section{Limitations and Discussion}
\label{sc:rcadns_discussion}

The proposed RCA-DNS architecture demonstrates that geographic considerations can be incorporated into the service selection process of anycast-based systems without introducing substantial performance penalties. By adding a regionalisation layer during DNS resolution, service providers can determine the set of replicas eligible to serve a given request while preserving the operational benefits traditionally associated with distributed service deployments.

From a regulatory perspective, RCA-DNS addresses one of the central challenges identified throughout this thesis: the lack of explicit geographic control in conventional anycast deployments. Whereas traditional anycast infrastructures delegate replica selection entirely to Internet routing dynamics, RCA-DNS introduces a mechanism through which service providers can define geographic constraints and steer users towards infrastructure deployed within a desired region. As a result, the architecture provides a practical foundation for improving geographic predictability and reducing the uncertainty associated with jurisdictional exposure.

Despite these advantages, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the effectiveness of the approach depends on the availability of a sufficient number of service replicas within the protected region. Regions with limited infrastructure deployments may experience reduced redundancy, lower resilience to failures, or higher latency compared with globally distributed deployments. Consequently, the achievable degree of geographic control remains closely tied to the underlying distribution of the service infrastructure.

Second, RCA-DNS operates at the service selection layer rather than at the network routing layer. While the mechanism can constrain the set of candidate replicas returned during DNS resolution, it cannot control the physical path subsequently followed by packets across the Internet. Routing decisions remain subject to the policies of autonomous systems, Internet service providers, and other external actors. Therefore, RCA-DNS provides control over the destination infrastructure selected for communication, but it does not guarantee that the entire network path remains confined to a specific geographic region.

Third, the current design assumes that traffic steering policies can be defined in terms of geographic regions. Although the evaluation presented in this chapter focuses on the European Economic Area, the architecture itself is not tied to any specific regional definition. Geographic policies can be updated as regulatory requirements, infrastructure deployments, or business objectives evolve. Nevertheless, future deployments may require more sophisticated policy models involving specific countries, bilateral agreements, legal adequacy decisions, or other jurisdictional considerations. Extending RCA-DNS to support richer policy definitions constitutes an interesting direction for future work.

The experimental evaluation also presents important limitations. The prototype deployment used service replicas exclusively located within the EEA and therefore focused on assessing the performance implications of geographic confinement rather than comparing against a globally distributed anycast deployment spanning multiple jurisdictions. Consequently, although the architecture is designed to reduce jurisdictional exposure, the experiments do not directly quantify the reduction that would be achieved in a real-world global deployment. Evaluating RCA-DNS under such conditions remains part of future work.

Similarly, the evaluation was conducted under controlled experimental conditions and does not capture all the operational complexities of large-scale production environments. Factors such as infrastructure failures, large routing incidents, highly dynamic traffic patterns, DNS cache behaviour, or long-term operational costs were outside the scope of the study and deserve further investigation.

Nevertheless, the proposed architecture demonstrates that geographic control and anycast are not inherently incompatible objectives. For decades, Internet-scale service deployments have prioritised performance, availability, and scalability, often treating geographic location as an indirect consequence of routing behaviour. RCA-DNS suggests that geographic considerations can instead become an explicit design parameter without requiring modifications to the underlying Internet routing infrastructure.

More broadly, this work supports the idea that future Internet services may increasingly require mechanisms capable of expressing and enforcing geographic constraints. As concerns regarding data sovereignty, international transfers, and jurisdictional exposure continue to grow, approaches such as RCA-DNS provide an initial step towards infrastructures that are not only performant and resilient but also geographically aware.
